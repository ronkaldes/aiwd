The lab — 6 steps, ~60–90 minutes

Do them in order; each builds on the last. You can stop after any step and still have something finished to show. Step 0 is only for people without a dataset.

0

No data? Generate a practice dataset

⏱ ~3 minOptionalNew chat is fine

Skip this if you have a real CSV or Excel file — just drop it into the kit's data/ folder. Otherwise, this prompt has Claude invent a realistic dataset with hidden patterns and flaws planted on purpose, plus a private answer key so you can check your work at the end.

Prompt · Step 0 
Generate a realistic synthetic CSV dataset I can use to practice data analysis.

Topic: [PICK ONE — e.g. "customers of a subscription software company"]
Outcome to hide in the data: [e.g. "which customers cancel"]

Requirements:
- About 800–1,200 rows and 20–35 columns; one row = one [customer/order/ticket/...].
- Mix of column types: identifiers, categories, dates, money, counts, scores, and a clear
  outcome column for the outcome above.
- Make it interesting to explore: plant 3–4 realistic hidden patterns that drive the outcome,
  plus a handful of data-quality flaws (some missing values, one duplicate row, a few
  impossible values, one extreme outlier).
- 100% synthetic — no real companies or people.

Give me the finished CSV file, plus a short list (for my eyes only) of the patterns
and flaws you planted, so I can check my work later.
What you should get back
  • A downloadable CSV — save it into the kit's data/ folder.
  • An answer key listing the planted patterns — keep it somewhere else and don't peek until Step 2 is done.
→ then Step 1
1

Look at your data

⏱ ~5 minNeeds: your data fileStarts the chat

You never analyze a file you don't understand. This first prompt asks Claude for the basics only — size, columns, and what one row means. Think of it as reading the label before you cook.

Prompt · Step 1 
I'm sharing a data file called [YOUR_FILE.csv]. In one or two sentences, here's what
I believe it is: [e.g. "an export of our customers — each row is one customer, with their plan, activity, and whether they're still with us"].

Before any analysis, just help me understand what I'm looking at:
1. How many rows and how many columns are there?
2. List every column with a short, plain-English explanation of what it means
   (your best interpretation — flag any column you're unsure about).
3. Show me 5 example rows in a readable table.
4. In one sentence, what does a single row represent?

Don't look for patterns or insights yet — I only want to understand the shape of the data.
Explain it like I'm new to data analysis.
📎
Before you hit send: click the paperclip in Claude.ai and attach your file. That's all "attaching" means — handing Claude the file to read.
📁
Nothing to attach: your file just needs to sit in the data/ folder. Claude Code opens it by name.
What a good answer looks like
  • Row & column counts that roughly match what you expected.
  • Every column explained in one plain line — with honest "(unsure)" flags. Correct those in your reply; Claude remembers for the rest of the chat.
  • A small table of 5 real rows and a one-sentence "one row = one ___". If that sentence is wrong, say so — everything later depends on it.
Optional follow-ups (paste as your next message)
Group the columns into a few simple categories (like "who/what this row is", "money", "activity", "dates", "outcomes") so it's easier to hold in my head.
Based on this data, what are the 3 most interesting questions it could answer? For each, name the outcome column I'd focus on. Help me pick one to pursue.

That second one matters — the rest of the lab keeps referring to "the outcome you care about." If you haven't picked one, this is where you do it (then update it in the setup box above 👆).

→ same chat, Step 2
2

Analyze the data & find the gaps

⏱ ~15 minSame chat as Step 1The heart of the lab

Now Claude acts like a senior data analyst: every flaw in the file, the headline numbers, what actually drives your outcome, and the surprises. Everything you build in Steps 3–6 stands on this answer.

Prompt · Step 2 
Now act as a senior data analyst and analyze [YOUR_FILE.csv] in depth. The outcome I care
about is [YOUR OUTCOME — e.g. "which customers cancelled", "which orders were returned"]. I want four things, with the actual numbers behind
every claim:

1. DATA QUALITY — Find every problem in the data: missing values, blanks, impossible values
   (numbers outside their sensible range, dates that can't be right), duplicates, and
   suspicious outliers. For each one, tell me how many rows are affected and how you'd clean
   or handle it.

2. THE BIG PICTURE — What are the headline numbers for my outcome (the overall rate, average,
   or total — whichever fits)? Then break it down by the 3–4 most natural grouping columns in
   this data (things like category, region, size, type, time period). Call out where it's
   surprisingly high or low.

3. DRIVERS — Which columns most separate the rows where the outcome happened from the rows
   where it didn't (or the high values from the low)? Rank the top drivers and show the
   difference in numbers for each.

4. ANOMALIES & SURPRISES — Find anything counterintuitive: rows that look one way on the
   surface but behave the opposite way, groups that break the overall pattern, or existing
   score/label columns in the data that disagree with what actually happened.

Finish with the 3 most important, non-obvious insights a manager should know, in plain
English. Describe everything you find as patterns in THIS dataset — don't overclaim beyond it.
What a good answer looks like
  • Counted flaws — every real dataset has some; finding them is normal, not failure.
  • A headline number you can sanity-check against what you know.
  • Ranked drivers with numbers — "rows with low X had the outcome 3× as often", not just "X matters".
  • At least one genuine surprise — often your best material for Steps 3–6.
  • Three plain-English takeaways a manager could act on tomorrow.
Optional follow-ups
Show me the 10 rows that most break the pattern — the ones that look fine on the surface but had the worst outcome. List their key numbers in a table.
For the group with the most surprising result, dig into WHY. Is it really that group, or is something else going on underneath?
Turn your top 3 insights into a one-paragraph summary I could read aloud to a manager in 30 seconds.
If something goes wrong
  • Numbers look off / Claude seems to guess: reply "Recompute directly from the file and show the row counts you used."
  • No obvious outcome column: reply "There's no single outcome column — suggest 3 outcomes we could derive from these columns, and analyze the best one."
  • Overwhelming answer: reply "Give me a short summary first — just the headlines."
→ same chat, Step 3
3

Build a stunning data-story page

⏱ ~10 minSame chat as Step 2You get: one .html file

Claude found the story — now it shows it: a polished one-file web page (like this one!) that opens with a double-click. No coding, no design tools. Make sure Step 2 finished with real numbers before sending this.

Prompt · Step 3 
Build me a single, self-contained HTML file (one file: inline CSS and JavaScript, no build step,
works by double-clicking it) that presents the key insights from [YOUR_FILE.csv] as a stunning,
modern, executive "data story" web page.

Include:
- A bold hero section with the single headline finding and the one number that matters most.
- The top drivers of the outcome shown as clean, labeled charts.
- The breakdowns by the groupings where the differences were biggest.
- A standout callout for the most surprising finding from the analysis — the thing nobody
  would have guessed.
- A short "what we should do about it" section at the end.

Make it beautiful: a polished dark theme, big readable typography, smooth scrolling, animated
number counters, and real chart visualizations (you can load a charting library like Chart.js
from a CDN). Use the actual numbers from the analysis so the values are correct. It should look
like something I'd be proud to put on screen in front of executives. Give me the finished HTML file.
What a good answer looks like + how to open it
  • A bold hero with your one headline number (often counting up), charts for the top drivers, and a highlighted callout for the surprise.
  • Check the numbers against Step 2 — they should match. If not: "use the exact numbers from your Step 2 analysis above, not estimates."
  • Claude.ai: it may preview live; download the file to keep it. Claude Code: say "save this as data-story.html in my folder", then double-click the file.
  • Charts need internet (they load Chart.js from a CDN); the rest works offline.
Optional follow-ups
Use my brand colors and add a subtle accent to the hero. My colors: [YOUR COLORS — or paste your brand cheatsheet from reference/BRAND_CHEATSHEET_TEMPLATE.md].
Add a small "data note" footer stating the source of the data, the row and column counts we analyzed, and the as-of date.
→ Step 4 (thinking, not spreadsheets)
4

Research the landscape & propose an MVP

⏱ ~15 minNo data file neededFill in your 3 findings

Turn insight into action. Claude scans how the wider world handles your problem, then pitches ONE small idea — an MVP (Minimum Viable Product: the smallest version that still delivers value). Before pasting, replace the three FINDING lines with your Step 2 results — or first ask Claude: "List our top 3 findings from the analysis in one line each." Tip: turn on web search in Claude.ai (the globe icon) if you have it.

Prompt · Step 4 
I analyzed our data about [YOUR TOPIC — e.g. "our customers and why they cancel"]. I want to turn insight into action.
Our key findings:
- [FINDING 1 from Step 2]
- [FINDING 2 from Step 2]
- [FINDING 3 from Step 2 — ideally the surprising one]

First, research how this problem is handled in the wider world. Who are the main players,
tools, or common approaches in [YOUR DOMAIN — e.g. "customer retention for subscription software"]? For each, what do they offer around
this problem, and what do their users praise or complain about?

Then tell me:
- Where does our current approach look strong, and where does it look behind?
- Based on that landscape AND our data findings, propose ONE focused MVP — a small feature,
  tool, or process change — we should build to act on these findings.

Describe the MVP in one tight paragraph: what it is, who it's for, the single problem it
solves, and why it would win. Keep it realistic and as minimal as possible — the smallest
thing that delivers the value.
What a good answer looks like
  • A quick landscape briefing — what others actually do about your problem, not just names.
  • Honest two-sided framing, tied back to your findings — especially the surprising one.
  • ONE focused MVP, not five. Got five? Reply: "Choose the single best one and drop the rest."
  • No web search? Reply: "No web access is fine — use what you know and flag anything I should double-check."
Optional follow-ups
Rewrite the MVP pitch for a busy executive: 4 sentences, plain language, no jargon.
What would the very first version look like, and what would we deliberately leave out of v1?
→ same chat, Step 5
5

Build the MVP prototype

⏱ ~10 minSame chat as Step 4Mock data only

The idea becomes a thing you can click. No placeholders here — "that MVP" refers to the idea already in your chat. The prototype uses made-up data on purpose, so it's safe to share with anyone.

Prompt · Step 5 
Now build a working prototype of that MVP as a single self-contained HTML file (inline CSS and
JavaScript, no backend, no real data — use a small amount of hardcoded mock data that you make up,
shaped like the real data we analyzed).

Keep it as minimal as possible: the smallest version that demonstrates the core idea end-to-end
and feels real to click through. Show the main screen a user would live in, make the key items
clickable, and when I click one, show WHY it's flagged/interesting and ONE recommended action.
Make sure the mock data includes a couple of examples of the surprising pattern we found in the
analysis, so the demo makes the insight visible.

Modern, clean, professional UI. It must work by just double-clicking the file. Put a short comment
at the very top of the file stating what it is and that all the data is fake sample data.
Give me the finished HTML file.
What a good answer looks like
  • A clean main screen with a handful of mock items shaped like your real data.
  • One or two items that demonstrate your surprising finding — the best ones to point at in a demo.
  • Click an item → a panel explains why it's flagged and gives one recommended action.
  • Blank page after double-clicking? Reply: "It opened blank in my browser. Please fix it so it works by just double-clicking, all in one file."
Optional follow-ups
Add a small summary bar at the top with the 3 numbers that matter most on this screen.
Make the flagged items stand out more — add a clear badge and sort them to the top of the list.
→ the finale
6

Create the management deck

⏱ ~15 minSame chat as Steps 2–5The finale

Tell the story to the people who can say "yes." No brand skill installed in your Claude? Fill in reference/BRAND_CHEATSHEET_TEMPLATE.md from the kit (5 min), copy its one-paragraph brief, and paste it above this prompt in the same message.

Prompt · Step 6 
Using the brand brief above, create a single self-contained HTML slide deck for
[YOUR COMPANY] leadership. One HTML file, no external files, in the brand colors, font,
and tone described in the brief. One idea per slide.

The story: in one short workshop we took our own data about [YOUR TOPIC], analyzed it
with AI, and found insights worth acting on. Walk leadership through it and ask for buy-in.

Cover, one idea per slide:
1. Title + the one-line message.
2. What we did (analyzed our own data with AI in an afternoon).
3. The headline findings, with the real numbers.
4. The blind spot: the surprising finding our current view of the business misses.
5. The root-cause story behind our worst-performing group — and why it's fixable.
6. How others handle this problem, and where we stand.
7. The MVP we propose — what it is and who it helps.
8. The ask: why we should invest, and the expected payoff.
9. Close on a confident recommendation.

Lead with outcomes, then the numbers.

What a good answer looks like
  • A 9-slide deck that reads like a confident story — not a wall of charts.
  • The same figures from Step 2, not new inventions. Deck forgot your numbers? Paste your key results back in and say "rebuild the deck using these exact numbers."
  • A standout slide on the surprising finding — the one leadership will remember.
  • Your colors, your font, your tone. Save the HTML file and double-click to present.
Optional follow-ups
On the "ask" slide, add one rough payoff estimate: if we fixed even a quarter of the problem the data shows, what would that be worth? Show your math simply.
Rewrite slide 1's one-line message three different ways — one for a CFO, one for a product leader, one for the CEO. Keep each under 12 words.
Write a 30-second spoken intro I can say out loud before slide 1, in a warm, confident tone.
